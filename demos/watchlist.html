Your Role

Verification Officer · Regional Police Operations Center

Transit-hub cameras feed a facial-recognition system called VigilMatch. Whenever VigilMatch thinks it has found someone on the regional watchlist, the case lands in your queue: a camera capture, the watchlist record it thinks matches, and a similarity verdict.

Your job is to verify. For each case you have three options: confirm the match, reject the match, or flag it for a second reviewer when you are not sure. You have 25 seconds per case. If the clock runs out, the queue does not wait: the case is auto-confirmed and moves on without you, the way a rubber stamp would.

Remote biometric identification is listed as high-risk in Annex III of the EU AI Act. Eight cases are waiting. Look closely: the system is right most of the time, which is exactly what makes the times it is wrong easy to miss.

The science

Skitka, Mosier and Burdick (1999) put people in front of an automated monitoring system and found that every single participant committed at least one commission error, acting on a bad automated cue, and 55% missed events the automation failed to flag at all. Time pressure was the multiplier: under a deadline, verification collapses into ratification. People stop checking the evidence and start checking their willingness to disagree with the machine.

The EU AI Act's answer to this is not a suggestion. Article 14(5) states that for real-time remote biometric identification, no action can be taken on a system's output unless that identification has been separately verified and confirmed by at least two natural persons with the necessary competence and authority. The law assumes exactly the failure mode this simulation may have just put you through: a single tired reviewer, a countdown clock, and a queue that keeps moving whether or not you looked.
