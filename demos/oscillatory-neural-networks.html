Oscillatory Neural Networks

A standard artificial neuron outputs a number. An oscillatory neuron is a small clock that never stops ticking. What it “says” is when it ticks compared with its neighbours, which engineers call its phase.

Connect many such clocks and they tug on each other’s timing. The answer of the network is the pattern of who ends up in step with whom. Two experiments below show the idea, then an assessment of whether it is good for anything.

1. From scattered ticking to one rhythm

Forty oscillators, each with its own preferred tempo, like fireflies that flash at slightly different speeds. Turn up the coupling: each one starts to nudge its timing towards the crowd. Nothing happens for a while, then the group snaps into step.

The Kuramoto model (1975). θ is where an oscillator is in its cycle, ω its own tempo, K the coupling. The pink arrow on the clock face is the average of all hands: its length is the synchrony number.

2. A memory made of timing

Now 64 oscillators with identical tempo, every one coupled to every other. The couplings were set from three stored pictures: cells that share a colour in a picture pull each other into step, cells that differ push each other half a beat apart. Damage a picture, let the network settle, and the timing repairs it.

dθi/dt = (1 / N) · Σj Jij sin(θj − θi) − Ks sin(2θi)

Jij is positive when cells i and j agree across the stored pictures and negative when they disagree (a Hebbian rule, as in a Hopfield network). The last term is a reference signal at twice the tempo that makes each oscillator choose one of two timings. No program runs the recall: the network slides into the nearest stable timing pattern by itself.

So, is this useful?

Short answer: an elegant idea with a long history, serious researchers and no product. Whether it becomes useful depends on two separate bets, and neither has paid off yet.

Why people are excited

  • Physics does the computing. Coupled oscillators settle into a stable state on their own, as you saw in part 2. Build them from tiny analog devices and recall or optimisation could cost far less energy than digital arithmetic. The EU project NeurONN (2020 to 2023) set out to build such chips from vanadium-dioxide oscillators and stated goals of up to 250× better energy efficiency than digital CMOS oscillators.
  • Hard puzzles as a landscape. The same settling can search for good answers to combinatorial problems such as splitting a graph. These are “oscillator Ising machines” (Wang and Roychowdhury, 2019).
  • Timing as an extra channel. Neurons that fire in step can mark “these features belong to the same object”. A deep-learning version, Artificial Kuramoto Oscillatory Neurons (AKOrN, an oral paper at ICLR 2025), reported gains in finding objects without labels, in Sudoku-style reasoning and in robustness to adversarial images.

Why people are sceptical

  • The hardware is tiny. Published chips couple a handful of oscillators: up to nine vanadium-dioxide oscillators in a 2024 Nature Communications paper, a two-by-two array in a 2025 proof of concept. Energy advantages are mostly goals and simulations, not measurements at scale.
  • Memory capacity is poor. Tick the fourth picture in part 2 and recall starts to fail. Classic oscillator memories store only a few patterns before they blur into blends.
  • Wiring and precision. Coupling everything to everything needs N² connections, and real analog oscillators drift in tempo. Both get worse as the network grows.
  • Ordinary computers are hard to beat. For optimisation, reviews of Ising machines have found no clear, general advantage over good classical algorithms on standard processors.
  • The software results run on GPUs. AKOrN is trained with backpropagation and simulated step by step, so it gains none of the hoped-for energy savings, and its accuracy on clean images trails a plain ResNet.

Where it stands at the end of 2026

The idea is half a century old (Kuramoto 1975; Hoppensteadt and Izhikevich proposed an oscillatory associative memory in 1999) and it borrows from a hypothesis about the brain, binding by synchrony, that neuroscientists themselves still dispute. It is having a second life in two places: analog hardware for low-power devices and optimisation, and oscillator layers inside otherwise normal deep networks.

For a practitioner: nothing here is something you can buy or deploy today, and nothing in it threatens transformers. It is worth knowing as a reminder that “a neuron outputs a number” is a design choice, not a law. If oscillators matter in practice, the first sign will be a chip that beats a microcontroller on a real task at a real size, or an oscillator layer that wins on a mainstream benchmark. Neither has happened yet.
